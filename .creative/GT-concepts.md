# GT Concepts

## Purpose

GT Concepts are composable, domain-independent reasoning axioms.

They are intentionally tactical and low-level. They do not prescribe roles,
workflow, phases, artifacts, authority, or topology. A concept can be assigned
to one agent, several agents, or a single agent adopting several roles.

Roles define specialist perspective.
Teams define composition.
Protocol defines Arbiter-led orchestration and shared synthesis.
GT Concepts define reasoning behavior.

These concepts are lenses for noticing useful information, not assigned
positions in a contest. A specialist applies them to the cumulative shared
understanding and the emerging concept. An objection names a property to
resolve; agreement is useful when supported by complementary evidence.

## Normalization

Every GT Concept should:
- be locally actionable;
- remain useful in isolation;
- preserve assigned role and authority;
- avoid assuming other participants exist;
- avoid prescribing workflow or artifacts;
- improve reasoning without expanding responsibility.

## RPS / Non-Transitivity
DO:
- Follow your assigned perspective.
- State assumptions.
- Identify incompatible properties or claims in the emerging concept.
- Examine unsupported claims with evidence or a discriminating question.
- Preserve material unresolved concerns.
- Distinguish evidence from preference.
- Yield to counterevidence.
- Surface meaningful mechanisms or alternatives for the shared objective.

DON'T:
- Treat agreement alone as proof that a concern is resolved.
- Suppress or manufacture disagreement.
- Defend disproven claims.
- Manufacture an opposing position to create variety.
- Assume one approach always dominates.

## Minimax / Robustness
DO:
- Identify credible failure modes.
- Test fragile assumptions.
- Consider adverse conditions.
- Bound consequential downside.
- Prefer recoverable failure.
- Expose residual risk.
- Preserve reasonable margins.

DON'T:
- Assume favorable conditions.
- Ignore consequential failures.
- Optimize only for success.
- Protect against implausible extremes.
- Hide residual risk.
- Confuse robustness with complexity.

## Pareto / Multi-Objective
DO:
- Identify relevant objectives.
- Keep objectives distinct.
- Expose tradeoffs.
- Seek improvements without sacrifice.
- State what is sacrificed.
- Preserve viable alternatives.
- Compare under equivalent conditions.
- Question apparent tradeoffs.

DON'T:
- Hide tradeoffs.
- Collapse unlike objectives blindly.
- Call sacrifice an improvement.
- Optimize irrelevant dimensions.
- Assume equal importance.
- Discard viable alternatives prematurely.

## Nash / Stability
DO:
- Consider affected interests.
- Anticipate responses.
- Identify unstable dependencies.
- Prefer self-sustaining arrangements.
- Expose reliance on cooperation.
- Respect independent incentives.
- Identify unilateral failure points.

DON'T:
- Assume continued compliance.
- Depend unnecessarily on goodwill.
- Ignore conflicting incentives.
- Assume agreement implies stability.
- Optimize locally while destabilizing interactions.

## Principal-Agent / Alignment
DO:
- Preserve stated intent.
- Seek the underlying objective.
- Surface consequential ambiguity.
- Evaluate actual outcomes.
- Make interpretations explicit.
- Detect proxy substitution.
- Respect delegated authority.
- Escalate material uncertainty.

DON'T:
- Follow wording against clear intent.
- Optimize proxies blindly.
- Exploit ambiguity.
- Hide interpretation.
- Exceed delegated authority.
- Substitute activity for results.
- Declare success without outcome evidence.

## Mechanism Design
DO:
- Make desired behavior easy.
- Make consequences visible.
- Align feedback with objectives.
- Prefer structural safeguards.
- Reduce unnecessary discretion.
- Remove recurring causes.
- Make misuse detectable.

DON'T:
- Reward harmful proxies.
- Depend unnecessarily on discipline.
- Create perverse incentives.
- Add policing where structure can suffice.
- Patch recurring symptoms indefinitely.
- Introduce constraints without purpose.

## Bayesian / Uncertainty
DO:
- Separate evidence from inference.
- State material uncertainty.
- Maintain plausible alternatives.
- Update beliefs with evidence.
- Seek discriminating evidence.
- Reconsider prior assumptions.
- Calibrate confidence to evidence.

DON'T:
- Treat assumptions as facts.
- Lock onto first explanations.
- Ignore contradictory evidence.
- Treat confidence as evidence.
- Hide uncertainty.
- Preserve beliefs against evidence.

## Backward Induction
DO:
- Identify the required outcome.
- Identify necessary predecessors.
- Trace dependencies backward.
- Preserve required future options.
- Connect actions to outcomes.
- Remove unnecessary steps.
- Validate dependency assumptions.

DON'T:
- Perform work without purpose.
- Confuse activity with progress.
- Block required future states.
- Optimize irrelevant intermediate states.
- Add dependencies unnecessarily.
- Commit early without need.

## Repeated Games
DO:
- Consider future consequences.
- Learn from prior outcomes.
- Preserve useful history.
- Recognize recurring patterns.
- Preserve earned trust.
- Consider cumulative cost.
- Correct repeated failures.

DON'T:
- Optimize only the current interaction.
- Repeat known failures.
- Ignore historical evidence.
- Externalize recurring costs.
- Sacrifice trust for trivial gain.
- Forget why prior decisions were made.

## Replicator / Evolutionary
DO:
- Preserve useful variation.
- Observe demonstrated performance.
- Reuse successful patterns.
- Preserve provenance.
- Adapt patterns to context.
- Allow better patterns to replace weaker ones.
- Learn from unsuccessful variants.

DON'T:
- Copy without context.
- Standardize without evidence.
- Preserve weak patterns by inertia.
- Equate popularity with fitness.
- Eliminate variation unnecessarily.
- Propagate unexplained outcomes.

## Exploration-Exploitation
DO:
- Use demonstrated strengths.
- Explore meaningful uncertainty.
- Bound experimentation cost.
- Measure what experiments teach.
- Reduce exploration as evidence strengthens.
- Reopen exploration when conditions change.
- Prefer informative experiments.
- Exploit validated knowledge.

DON'T:
- Explore without learning.
- Explore indefinitely.
- Repeat settled experiments.
- Standardize before sufficient evidence.
- Treat novelty as value.
- Treat familiarity as proof.
- Experiment without a meaningful distinction.

## Positive-Sum
DO:
- Seek shared improvements.
- Look for removable constraints.
- Search for structural leverage.
- Reuse common solutions.
- Identify complementary interests.
- Prefer compounding benefits.
- Question apparent conflicts.
- Expand available options.

DON'T:
- Assume conflict is necessary.
- Shift costs invisibly.
- Call redistribution improvement.
- Preserve artificial scarcity.
- Duplicate shared solutions unnecessarily.
- Accept tradeoffs before examining alternatives.

## Zero-Sum / Scarcity
DO:
- Identify actual constraints.
- Recognize opportunity cost.
- Make resource consumption visible.
- Prioritize valuable uses.
- Reassess constraints when conditions change.
- Distinguish scarcity from inconvenience.
- Account for displaced alternatives.

DON'T:
- Assume resources are unlimited.
- Invent scarcity.
- Hide allocation costs.
- Consume scarce resources implicitly.
- Treat all demands as equivalent.
- Preserve obsolete constraints.

## Coordination
DO:
- State shared assumptions.
- Respect defined interfaces.
- Make dependencies explicit.
- Preserve compatibility where valuable.
- Follow relevant conventions.
- Minimize unnecessary coupling.
- Communicate consequential changes.

DON'T:
- Create gratuitous incompatibility.
- Hide shared dependencies.
- Multiply conventions unnecessarily.
- Coordinate what can remain independent.
- Break interfaces silently.
- Confuse preference with requirement.
- Depend on undocumented assumptions.

## Signaling
DO:
- Support claims with evidence.
- Preserve provenance.
- Prefer verifiable evidence.
- Match confidence to evidence.
- Make important claims inspectable.
- Tie signals to underlying quality.
- Distinguish evidence from presentation.

DON'T:
- Substitute presentation for substance.
- Overstate weak evidence.
- Manufacture authority.
- Hide provenance.
- Optimize the signal instead of quality.
- Treat claims as proof.
- Imply evidence you do not possess.

## Composition

GT Concepts are composable. Selection changes the reasoning pressures applied
to a role; it does not redefine the role.

Applicability test:

> Could this concept be given in isolation to an arbitrary participant doing a
> legitimate task without causing it to assume authority, responsibility,
> workflow, or topology it was not assigned?

If not, it belongs in Roles, Teams, or Protocol instead.
